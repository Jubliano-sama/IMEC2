## The Living Vitality Hub
The Living Vitality Hub serves as a testing ground where companies deploy and validate environmental equipment. Currently, the facility is outfitted with a dense sensor network measuring $CO_2$, $PM_{2.5}$, light levels, and occupancy. It also incorporates specialized recovery zones, including relaxation beds, to study their impact on cognitive performance. This ecosystem fosters a symbiotic relationship: the Hub gains access to cutting-edge technology, while companies receive empirical data to refine their products or support marketing claims.
## The Objective: Bridging Environment and Behavior
To deepen this research, I am developing a specialized feedback device designed to quantify the human element of the workspace. While environmental sensors provide "hard" data, this portable "clicker" captures real-time behavioral metrics. For example, a worker might be prompted to click when engaging in social interaction. Or when their environment is experienced as too hot or cold.

#### AI Study Summary
**Device Description** The study prototypes **Click-IO**, a tangible, mobile, and privacy-sensitive experience sampling tool designed to collect contextual data on office well-being. The system consists of a central "clicker station" equipped with an iPad that displays a daily challenge (e.g., "How many times did you lose your concentration?") and a set of mechanical clickers. To use the system, office workers pick up a clicker, scan it at the station to opt-in, and carry it with them throughout the day. They track their responses to the challenge by physically clicking the device. To capture spatial data, the system includes "localization poles" distributed across different office zones (e.g., meeting rooms, standing desks), where users scan their clickers to log their current location. The design prioritizes privacy by avoiding digital identification or the use of personal smartphones, ensuring all data collection is anonymous.

**Study and Conclusions** The device was evaluated during a 20-day deployment in a shared office environment in the Netherlands, where it was used alongside environmental sensors measuring variables like CO2, temperature, and noise. The study aimed to assess the user experience of the device and explore how subjective human feedback (clicker data) could complement objective environmental metrics. The researchers concluded that Click-IO successfully enabled "in-the-moment" reflection, allowing workers to capture accurate feedback without relying on memory, which is a common flaw in annual surveys. Furthermore, combining clicker data with sensor data provided a more holistic view of office well-being; for instance, the study revealed discrepancies where sensors indicated a comfortable environment, yet users reported frequent loss of concentration, highlighting that sensor data alone is insufficient for understanding worker experience.

**Strengths and Weaknesses** The primary strengths of Click-IO were its high level of privacy and its ability to foster social interaction. Participants appreciated the anonymity of the mechanical clickers, and the central station became a social hub that sparked conversations about well-being and office behavior. The device was also praised for being unobtrusive and easy to use. However, the study identified several weaknesses, particularly regarding the localization polls; users often forgot to scan their clickers when moving between zones, leading to gaps in spatial data. Additionally, the mechanical nature of the clickers meant the system could not record exact timestamps for individual clicks, limiting the granularity of the data. Participants also noted that some challenges were open to interpretation and expressed a desire for the system to visualize the collected data back to them, rather than just acting as an input device.
### The First Electronic Attempt

^2416df

The second iteration, dubbed "Smart Clicker 2.0," transitioned the project from mechanical counters to a connected IoT ecosystem. The hardware architecture consists of portable "Clicker" units and fixed "Anchor" stations, both built on Arduino UNO R4 microcontrollers. Unlike the passive predecessors, these devices actively communicate via Ultra-Wideband (UWB) to triangulate the user's position relative to the anchors. When a user interacts with the clicker, the device captures the event and transmits it over WiFi using secure MQTT protocols. This setup successfully introduces the temporal granularity missing from the mechanical study, allowing the system to log the precise second of every input alongside its spatial coordinates.

To visualize this data, a web-based dashboard was developed using Angular to serve as the project's command center. This frontend displays the "Question of the Day" and utilizes interactive heatmaps to overlay click density directly onto office floor plans, effectively bridging the gap between user behavior and physical location. The platform allows administrators to manage multiple office locations, upload floor plan images, and visually map anchors to physical coordinates using a grid interface. This solved the previous study's issue of users forgetting to "check in" at specific zones, as the localization is now handled by the system's anchor mapping rather than manual scanning.

Supporting the user interface is a backend infrastructure hosted on Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS), deployed using Terraform for consistent Infrastructure-as-Code management. The system enforces strict Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) integrated with Microsoft Entra ID, ensuring that data is securely siloed by tenant and office. The backend also handles automatic device provisioning; when a new clicker enters the network, it securely registers itself via the Device Provisioning Service (DPS) and retrieves its configuration (such as WiFi credentials) from digital "device twins," eliminating the need for hard-coded firmware updates.

Despite these significant advancements in software architecture and signal processing, the prototype remains a "naked" solution. The current iteration successfully proves the engineering logic—firmware, cloud infrastructure, and PCB connectivity—but it lacks a physical enclosure. It consumes too much power, requires complex setup and necessitates a fine WiFi network. The devices currently exist as exposed microcontrollers and sensors without an ergonomic or protective shell. This absence of industrial design limits immediate real-world deployment, as the delicate electronics are not yet housed in a form factor suitable for daily handling by office workers, leaving the tactile and aesthetic experience as a critical outstanding requirement.
## The Ambition
This project should leverage electronic integration to capture richer data. By tracking the spatial location of these clicks, we can analyze how specific room layouts or furnishings facilitate or hinder office behavior. Furthermore, correlating these manual inputs with ambient data—such as temperature or humidity—allows us to observe how the environment dictates social friction.

**Current scope addition**: Automated anchor self-setup. The system can now discover and solve the 3D geometry of the anchor network using only measured anchor-to-anchor distances plus an approximate minimum radio radius. This removes the need for manual anchor positioning surveys.

To achieve this, the proposed device must transcend the limitations of the mechanical prototype. The Click-IO study explicitly noted that the lack of timestamps prevented researchers from knowing exactly when an event occurred, limiting the analysis to aggregate counts per session. By integrating a microcontroller, this project aims to log the precise millisecond of every input. This temporal granularity is critical; it allows us to overlay human feedback directly onto high-frequency environmental data, revealing if a specific spike in noise or $CO_2$ correlates with an immediate loss of concentration.

Furthermore, the mechanical system relied on manual scanning at "localization poles," a process that introduced significant user error as participants frequently forgot to check in when moving between zones. The ambition is to automate this process through wireless localization. This ensures spatial context is captured passively and accurately without disrupting the user's workflow or relying on their memory. Finally, participants in the previous study expressed a desire for the data to be communicated back to them to support self-reflection, rather than just serving as a data extraction tool.
